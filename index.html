How can we study the mind?

Psychology is the study of the human mind and behaviour.

The mind itself is hard to study directly, because it doesn't always produce results we can measure. That's why behavioural neuroscientists and psychologists look at both the biology of the brain and the behaviours we can observe, to build a clearer picture of how the mind works.

What this tells us

This happens because of something called selective attention. When your brain concentrates hard on one task, like counting passes, it filters out other information to avoid getting overloaded. Attention is limited. If you're trying to listen to your teacher while checking messages, thinking about something else, or switching between lots of tasks, your brain has to divide its attention. You might feel like you're following the lesson, but you can actually miss important information.
It also explains why “multi-tasking” is misleading. Your brain doesn't really do several demanding tasks at the same time very well, it rapidly switches between them. Each switch can mean that you miss or forget information.
You don't always notice what you're not paying attention to.
This is why giving your brain a chance to focus on one thing at a time can make learning easier. When you're studying, listening to instructions or learning something new, reducing distractions gives your attention more of a chance to do its job.

How good is your memory?

Your working memory is the part of your mind that holds information while you're actually using it e.g. the phone number you repeat to yourself until you type it in, or the start of a sentence while your teacher finishes it. It's fast, but it's small.

Now try chunking

Working memory can only hold a handful of items, so the trick is to make each item bigger.
That's called chunking: grouping small pieces of information into fewer, larger units.
Example: A phone number is easier as 0412 998 341 than as ten separate digits, and "cat, dog, horse" can be stored as one idea (animals) instead of three.
Your brain is still holding about four things, but each thing now carries much more inside it. Chunking works best when the groups mean something to you.
Try the chunking technique: the next list is already sorted into groups. Try to remember the groups, not the words.

Step 3

Now try a mind palace

A mind palace, also known as method of loci, uses your spatial awareness to carry things it finds hard, like lists. Your brain is good at remembering places.
Build your own mind palace:
1. Pick a route you know without thinking: your walk from the front door to your bedroom, or your path between classes.
2. Then place each word somewhere along it and make the image strange. A glacier melting on the kitchen bench sticks; a glacier "in the kitchen" doesn't. To recall the list, walk the route in your head and collect what you left there. Ancient Greek orators used this to deliver hour-long speeches without notes.
